# KDE Codebook

## Task

- Read one abstract and list its knowledge components: the phenomena, principles, means and purposes that the paper relies on or produces.
- Use what you know to understand and classify what the abstract states, but do not add components it does not state.
- Not every abstract contains all four types.
- Leave a type empty when the abstract does not state it.
- Code components whether the paper introduces them or uses existing ones.

## Knowledge components schema

The schema is based on Arthur's idea that all technologies harness a phenomenon to address a human need (Arthur, 2009, "The nature of technology", Free Press). The bow and arrow was a technology that harnessed the phenomenon of elasticity to address the need for hunting from a safe distance. More specifically, Arthur argues that a technology fulfills a purpose by exploiting a phenomenon through a principle of use, carried out by means that are themselves technologies (Arthur 2007, "The structure of invention", Research Policy).

**Phenomenon**
- Definition: A natural or emergent regularity, including how it comes about.
- Deciding question: Would this happen without anyone designing it, whether in nature or across many agents?
- Example: "a DNA strand binds to its exact matching strand"
- Not this: Results from one sample ("Fifty-two isolates possessed the CRISPR"); an organism named on its own

**Principle**
- Definition: The idea of using a phenomenon for a purpose.
- Deciding question: Is this a general idea of how an effect is put to use, rather than a specific technique?
- Example: "use a matching DNA strand as bait"
- Not this: A named technique you could follow directly (a means); the purpose in the same phrase; an idea the text implies but doesn't state

**Means**
- Definition: Something concrete that does work in a technology the text describes or uses.
- Deciding question: Could you pick it up or follow it as described, like a molecule, strain, device, method or protocol?
- Example: "PCR test"
- Not this: Study design ("we examined CRISPR diversity in 94 isolates"); organisms or samples that are only studied

**Purpose**
- Definition: A need that a technology serves, technical or human.
- Deciding question: Is this an end that some technology delivers?
- Example: "diagnosing infections"
- Not this: The authors' research aim ("To understand co-evolutionary dynamics")

## Tie-break rules

Use these when two types both seem to fit.

1. **Is it a phenomenon, or something people use?** CRISPR, plasmids and enzymes can be part of the bacterium in one sentence and a tool in the next, so decide each time one appears. Ask who is doing the work. If it's the bacterium (fighting off a virus, say), it's a phenomenon. If it's researchers (editing a gene or detecting a pathogen), it's a means. Biology often writes as if cells and molecules had goals ("uses spacers to target", "regulates", "controls"). That wording doesn't make it a principle or a purpose. If nobody designed it, it's a phenomenon.
2. **Means or purpose?** Words like "typing", "detection" or "differentiation" can name either a tool or a job. If it's something you could run or follow, like a system, assay or analysis, it's a means. If it's a result someone wants, it's a purpose.

## What to record

One row per component:
- **Component type:** phenomenon, principle, means or purpose.
- **Label:** the component in a few words of your own.
- **Evidence:** the shortest exact words from the abstract that support it. Each phrase gets one type only, and evidence for different types must not overlap.
- **One component per distinct thing.** If a sentence states one regularity twice (that it happens, then how strongly), code it once. If it lists several distinct named items, code each one.
- **Confidence:** how sure you are of the type. *High:* the text states it plainly and only one type fits. *Medium:* it fits, but you needed a tie-break, or the text hedges it ("may", "suggests", "predicted"). *Low:* you're unsure it's a component at all, or unsure of its type.

## Worked example (fictitious)

| Component type | Label | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Phenomenon | Acquired immunity via virus-derived spacers | "Strains that survive a virus attack copy a piece of the virus's DNA into a special stretch of their own DNA as a new spacer, and from then on resist that virus" |
| Phenomenon | Strain-unique spacer patterns | "each strain's spacer pattern is unique" |
| Principle | Expose and select for resistance | "deliberately exposing starter cultures to a virus and keeping the survivors" |
| Principle | Spacer pattern as strain barcode | "serve as a barcode" |
| Means | DNA sequencing | "DNA sequencing" |
| Means | Virus-resistant starter cultures | "virus-resistant cultures" |
| Purpose | Industrial yogurt production | "industrial yogurt production" |
| Purpose | Distinguish bacterial strains | "telling strains apart" |
